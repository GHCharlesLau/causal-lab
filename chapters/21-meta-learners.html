01 the setup

Debias and estimate

Two jobs
at once.

Meta-learners are off-the-shelf ML models arranged to estimate a CATE. Here they must also undo the selection in the training data.

The training data is not randomized: only 22.5% of customers received email 1, and the treated look different from the control. The randomized test set has a 50.4% treated share, which makes it a clean evaluation sample.

Every learner predicts a CATE, then the test gain curve checks whether the ranking finds the responsive customers. The train curves are shown only as an overfitting check; they are computed on a biased sample.

The family

S uses one model; T uses one model per treatment; X imputes effects, fits second-stage models, and weights them by propensity. All three are built from ordinary supervised learners.

the test

Randomized data is the referee. Without it, a learner could fit the selection and look good on its own training set.

the trade

More structure can reduce bias, but it can also add variance. There is no free lunch; the gain curve is the judge.

02 s-learner

One model

The treatment
as a feature.

Fit one model of the outcome with treatment included, then difference the predictions at T = 1 and T = 0.

The S-learner is the simplest: a single model M(X, T). Its CATE prediction is M(X,1) − M(X,0). It handles continuous and discrete treatments and is a sensible first bet.

Its weakness is regularization: if the treatment is a weak predictor, the model may shrink its coefficient toward zero. Here, the S-learner’s test CATE never goes below +0.006 — it cannot find a single negative responder, even though other learners do.

s-learner τ(x) = M(x, 1) − M(x, 0)

The zero-bias trap

The S-learner can shrink the treatment effect toward zero, or drop the treatment entirely. Greater regularization makes it worse.

when it wins

When the treatment is strong and the data is plentiful. Simplicity is an advantage when the alternative is overfitting.

the evidence

Minimum predicted CATE is +0.006, not negative. That is the shrinkage signature, not proof that no customer is harmed.

03 t-learner

One model per treatment

Split by
the switch.

Fit a model for the treated and a model for the control, then subtract their predictions.

The T-learner forces the treatment into the structure: M0(X) for the control and M1(X) for the treated. The CATE is their difference. It avoids dropping a weak treatment variable.

But each model sees only part of the data. When one group is much smaller, that model is heavily regularized, and the difference can be biased. Here the treated share is only 22.5%, so this is a real risk.

t-learner τ(x) = M1(x) − M0(x)

The imbalance trap

If one treatment group has few observations, its model is simple while the other is flexible. The difference then invents heterogeneity that is not there.

the range

T-learner test CATE runs from −0.133 to 0.374. It finds negative responders the S-learner misses.

the overfit

Train gain is much higher than test gain; fitting two models on halves of the data makes overfitting easier.

04 x-learner

Two stages and a propensity

Learn from
the imputation.

Use the first-stage models to impute effects, fit second-stage models on those imputations, and weight by propensity.

The X-learner starts like the T-learner: M0 and M1. Then it imputes the effect for each unit using the model from the other group: for the control, M1(X) − Y; for the treated, Y − M0(X).

It fits Mτ0 and Mτ1 to those imputed effects, then combines them with the propensity score: τ = e(X)Mτ0 + (1−e(X))Mτ1. The model trained where treatment was more likely gets more weight.

x-learner τ(x) = e(x)Mτ0(x) + (1 − e(x))Mτ1(x)

The fix

When treated units are scarce, the second stage can borrow strength from the large control group. The propensity weight favors the model estimated where treatment is common.

the result

X-learner test CATE runs from −0.094 to 0.418 and has the highest top-decile gain, 1.9%.

the cost

More moving parts means more variance and more ways to misspecify. It is not automatically better.

05 the comparison

Same test, three curves

Close,
not identical.

All three learners are evaluated on the randomized test set with the same gain curve.

The three curves are close. The X-learner has the highest top-decile gain at 1.9%, the S-learner 1.5%, and the T-learner 1.2%. The overall effect is 6.85%.

The differences are small. On another dataset or with different base learners, the ranking could flip. The lesson is the menu, not a universal winner.

the book's reading

The book calls the three learners “pretty similar” on this dataset. Our reproduced curves agree; the X-learner's edge is small.

the warning

Do not pick a learner from one leaderboard. Base model, hyperparameters, and treatment imbalance all matter.

06 key ideas

Carry these forward

Four things
to keep.

  1. S-learner: one model. Treatment is a feature; simple and general, but can shrink the effect toward zero or drop it.
  2. T-learner: two models. One per treatment level; avoids dropping the treatment but struggles when groups are imbalanced.
  3. X-learner: two stages. Impute effects, fit second-stage models, and weight by propensity; useful when one group is scarce.
  4. Evaluate on randomized data. The gain curve is the referee; the train curve on biased data is only an overfit alarm.

The sentence to avoid

“The X-learner is the best meta-learner.” It is one option in a menu; the right choice depends on the data, the treatment balance, and the base model.

to remember

Start simple. Try the S-learner, then check whether the treatment signal survives. Add structure only when the diagnosis calls for it.

07 practice

Check yourself

Can you
defend it?

Four short questions. Answer first, then open the field note.

01 Why can the S-learner miss negative effects?

Regularization shrinks the treatment coefficient toward zero. If the treatment is a weak predictor, the model may predict a nearly constant positive effect and never a negative one.

02 When does the T-learner struggle?

When one treatment group is much smaller. That group's model is heavily regularized, so the difference between the two models can be biased.

03 What does the propensity score do in the X-learner?

It weights the two second-stage models, favoring the one estimated in the group where treatment was more likely and data was more plentiful.

04 Why evaluate on randomized data?

On biased data, the group difference is confounded. Randomized treatment makes the gain curve a credible ranking test.
