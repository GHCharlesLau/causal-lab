01 the hammer

Prediction is not optimization

A good forecast
can be a bad policy.

The five-line ML recipe predicts an outcome. It does not tell you which action changes the outcome.

The business question is “how much coupon should each customer get?” The predictive question is “what net value will this customer produce?” Those are different targets. A model can be excellent at the second and useless at the first.

The coupon also enters net value as a cost. If the model predicts net value well, it has already absorbed the coupon’s effect. Segmenting on those predictions removes the variation needed to compare coupon values.

target shift E[Y|X]   →   E[Y(t) − Y(t′) | X]

The thumb

If all you have is a prediction model, every business problem looks like a forecasting problem. Coupon optimization is not one.

the R²

The model scored 0.538 on train and 0.505 on test. That is decent forecasting, and irrelevant to the policy comparison.

the cost

Coupons are subtracted from net value. A model that predicts net value is partly predicting the coupon assignment, not the coupon effect.

02 the coupon data

Not randomly assigned

Age drove
the coupons.

Older customers got larger coupons. The raw coupon–net-value correlation is negative, and it is not causal.

The coupon counts are heavily concentrated at 5 and 10 reais. The correlation between age and coupon value is 0.897; the correlation between coupon value and net value is −0.078. Both are signs of assignment bias, not a causal effect.

This note sidesteps the bias by using the book’s simulated data, where the true counterfactual policy can be regenerated. The lesson is still about prediction versus causality, not about this particular dataset.

The correlation trap

“More coupons, lower net value” is not evidence that coupons hurt. Older customers get both larger coupons and lower net value, so age confounds the raw correlation.

the simple policy

Look at the average net value by coupon value and give everyone the best one. Here, 5 reais wins with 252.93 per customer.

the artifact

The simulated data lets us regenerate the outcome under any coupon policy. Real data would not give us that counterfactual.

03 the policy bench

Simple vs model

The flat rule
wins.

Give everyone 5 reais, or use the predicted-net-value bands to choose a coupon. The flat rule earns more.

the model policy

Ten prediction bands, each with its own best coupon. The choices vary: 5, 10, and 15 reais.

the result

The apparent personalization loses 22.99 per customer. Prediction bands are not causal segments.

04 why it fails

The flattening

A good model
eats the slope.

Within a prediction band, net value barely varies. If the outcome does not vary, it cannot reveal how the coupon changes it.

Prediction models partition the outcome axis. By construction, customers in the same band have similar predicted net value. That is what makes the model good; it is also what removes the variation needed to estimate the coupon effect inside the band.

Excluding the coupon from the model’s features is not enough. Features that drive coupon assignment can carry the treatment information back into the prediction, so the model still learns the coupon pattern indirectly.

Bad control, new clothes

Segmenting on a prediction of the outcome is like conditioning on a proxy for the treatment-outcome path. It blocks the variation the policy needs.

Coupon chosen by the model, per prediction band

the exception

Prediction can help when outcome and elasticity move together, so slicing on Y also slices on the effect. Coupons are not that case.

the fix

Target the derivative, not the level. That is the whole book: estimate dY/dT, then optimize.

05 when it helps

The rare exception

When outcome
and effect align.

If higher outcomes systematically imply higher or lower sensitivity, a prediction slice can also be an effect slice.

Waiting time is the clean example. As waiting increases, satisfaction falls, and the marginal drop is largest at the start. Both the outcome and the marginal effect move in the same direction, so a prediction model can accidentally group similar elasticities.

Coupons are the opposite. Net value rises and then falls with the coupon value, so the same outcome level can occur on both sides of the optimum. A prediction band mixes customers with positive and negative elasticities.

Coupons

Hump-shaped

Outcome and elasticity reverse signs. Prediction slices mix both sides of the optimum.

Waiting time

Monotone

Outcome and elasticity move together. Prediction slices are more likely to group similar effects.

The safe rule

Do not assume a predictive model can rank treatment effects. Check the shape of the response curve; if it reverses, prediction will mix the signs.

the graphical view

Conditioning on a prediction of Y blocks part of the path from T to Y. With random T, it only removes variance; with confounded T, it can remove the effect.

the bottom line

Prediction is a legitimate tool for forecasting. It is not a policy optimizer.

06 key ideas

Carry these forward

Four things
to keep.

  1. Predicting Y is not optimizing Y. A policy question needs the derivative of the outcome with respect to the treatment, not the level.
  2. Prediction bands flatten the slope. Within a band, outcome variance is small by construction, so the coupon effect cannot be seen.
  3. Leaving T out of the model is not enough. Features that drive treatment assignment can carry T’s information back into the prediction.
  4. Prediction helps only when outcome and effect align. Monotone response curves can be sliced safely; hump-shaped ones cannot.

The sentence to avoid

“The model has a high R², so its coupon policy must be good.” The policy uses the model’s ranking, not its fit, and the ranking can be economically backwards.

to remember

Ask what decision the model changes. If the decision is a treatment level, the target is the treatment effect.

07 practice

Check yourself

Can you
defend it?

Four short questions. Answer first, then open the field note.

01 Why did the model policy lose money?

It grouped customers by predicted net value, which flattened the within-group variation in net value. The best coupon per band was chosen from too little signal.

02 Why is the simple policy hard to beat?

It directly compares net value across coupon values and picks the best average. It uses the treatment variation that the prediction bands remove.

03 Does removing the coupon from the model fix it?

No. Features that predict coupon assignment can carry the treatment information indirectly, so the model still learns the coupon pattern.

04 When can prediction be useful for a causal policy?

When outcome and elasticity move together, as with monotone waiting-time effects. Then prediction slices can group similar elasticities. Hump-shaped responses violate this.
