01 the problem

No ground truth

You cannot
score a slope.

A predictive model is judged against observed outcomes. A sensitivity model is judged against a quantity no one can observe.

We can’t see an individual price sensitivity. What we can see, with randomized treatment, is the sensitivity of a group. That is enough to evaluate an ordering.

Sort the days by a model’s prediction, then estimate the price slope in the top 10%, the top 20%, and so on. A useful model produces a curve that separates from the random benchmark before it converges to the overall average.

Why random data

With randomized prices, a group slope is an unbiased estimate of that group’s average sensitivity. With non-random prices, the same slope inherits confounding.

the trick

Evaluate the ordering, not the individual prediction. A model that ranks the sensitive days near the top is useful even if its levels are noisy.

the cost

Random data is expensive. You do not set prices at random for fun; you do it to learn. The evaluation budget is a business decision.

02 three models

Causal, predictive, random

Three ways
to rank a day.

One model predicts sensitivity; one predicts sales; one predicts noise. All three are judged with the same curve.

Causal: a linear model with price × context interactions, trained on non-random prices. Its sensitivity prediction is the derivative of the fitted surface.

Predictive: a gradient-boosted sales model, trained on the same non-random data. Train R² 0.9252, randomized test R² 0.7711.

Random: a uniform score. It is a benchmark: if an evaluation metric rewards the random model, the metric is not measuring ranking skill.

the benchmark

The R² trap

A high R² says the model knows the sales level. It says nothing about whether the model knows which days are price-sensitive. Those are different targets.

the test

The predictive model was trained on non-random prices and tested on random ones, so some performance drop is expected. It is still a strong sales forecast.

the question

Which model puts the most price-sensitive days at the top? That is the question the gain curve answers.

03 sensitivity bands

Ten equal bands

The shape
of the ranking.

Split each model’s predictions into ten equal bands and estimate the price slope in each band.

A good ranker produces a clear gradient: the top band should have a different slope from the bottom. The random model should be flat, up to noise. The causal model’s first bands are extremely price-sensitive; the predictive model’s bands are more evenly ordered but less extreme.

What to look for

Spread: do the bands differ? Order: do they move monotonically? Extremes: does the model isolate the most sensitive days?

the book's reading

The causal model finds the extreme sensitive days but its ordering is uneven. The predictive model orders better but misses the extremes. Neither is uniformly better.

the next step

Bands are a first look. The cumulative gain curve gives a sharper preference statement.

04 cumulative gain

The curve that compares

Gain, not
accuracy.

Cumulative gain multiplies the group slope by the share of days included. The random benchmark is the diagonal from zero to the ATE.

At each fraction k/N, the curve is the sensitivity of the top k days times k/N. Every model ends at the same ATE; the question is where it sits in between.

the benchmark

The dashed diagonal is the random model. Any curve that hugs it is not adding ranking information.

the caution

The cumulative gain is an evaluation on random data. Without randomization, the group slopes would be confounded and the curve would not be trustworthy.

05 key ideas

Carry these forward

Four things
to keep.

  1. Sensitivity is unobservable, but group sensitivity is estimable. Random treatment makes the group slope an unbiased evaluation target.
  2. Evaluate the ordering. A CATE model is useful if it puts responsive units near the top, not if it predicts each slope perfectly.
  3. Cumulative gain is the ROC of targeting. It compares the top-k sensitivity with a random benchmark; all curves converge to the ATE.
  4. R² is not enough. A sales forecast can be excellent and still rank sensitivity poorly.

The sentence to avoid

“The model has a high R², so it is a good causal model.” The R² measures the level, not the slope, and the model may be ranking the wrong days.

to remember

Keep a random sample for evaluation. It is the only place where the group slopes are credible without extra assumptions.

06 practice

Check yourself

Can you
defend it?

Four short questions. Answer first, then open the field note.

01 Why can’t we validate sensitivity at the unit level?

We never observe the same unit under both treatment conditions, so the individual slope is counterfactual. We can estimate a group slope with randomized treatment.

02 What does cumulative gain add over sensitivity by band?

It evaluates the entire ordering, not just ten arbitrary bands, and it gives a common benchmark: the diagonal from zero to the ATE.

03 Why include a random model?

It is a placebo. If the evaluation metric rewards a random ranking, the metric is not capturing ranking skill.

04 Why is randomization essential for the evaluation?

Without it, the group price slope is confounded by other determinants of sales. Randomized prices make the group slope an unbiased sensitivity estimate.
