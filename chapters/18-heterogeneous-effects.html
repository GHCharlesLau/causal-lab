01 from ATE to CATE

From one number to a function

Who responds
differently?

The average treatment effect tells you whether to treat. The conditional average treatment effect tells you who to treat.

A price increase is a treatment. Each day is a unit. The outcome is sales; the context is temperature, weekday, and cost. The ATE is one slope for everyone. The CATE allows that slope to change with the context.

This is personalization in causal terms: not predicting who buys the most, but predicting who responds most to the treatment. The two questions are different, and they can produce different policies.

ATE → CATE E[Y1 − Y0]   →   E[Y1 − Y0 | X]

We cannot see an individual sensitivity directly; it is a counterfactual slope. But with randomized treatment and a model that interacts the treatment with context, the fitted derivative becomes an estimate of the CATE.

The two decisions

Program evaluation: should we treat at all? Personalization: who should receive which treatment? The first needs an ATE; the second needs heterogeneity.

the lever

Price is a treatment you can set. Temperature is context you cannot. The interaction is what makes the response differ by day.

the catch

Sensitivity is not observed. The model predicts it from a fitted outcome surface; the ranking is the useful part, not the exact level.

02 the CATE bench

Sensitivity bands

One price,
two responses.

Predict sensitivity with an interaction model, split the test days at the median, and fit a separate price-sales line in each band.

The first model has no interaction: it predicts the same price sensitivity, −2.75, for every day. The second adds price × temperature; the third adds price interactions with every context feature. The third model gives a different sensitivity to each day.

Split the test set at the median predicted sensitivity. In the low-sensitivity band, the raw price slope is −5.73; in the high-sensitivity band, it is +0.88 — essentially flat. The average slope is −2.34.

the split

The median predicted sensitivity is almost exactly zero. The low band contains the days the model thinks will fall; the high band contains the days it thinks will barely move.

the caveat

The band slopes are raw test-set slopes, not causal estimates. They show the partition is informative, not that the model is calibrated.

03 prediction vs sensitivity

A good prediction is not enough

It predicts sales,
not slopes.

A gradient-boosted model predicts sales very well — test R² 0.9124. But split the days by predicted sales, and the price slopes barely change.

The predictive model separates high-sales days from low-sales days. That is exactly what it was trained to do. It does not separate high-sensitivity days from low-sensitivity days, because sales level and price response are different objects.

The contrast is the lesson of the chapter: a model can be excellent at E[Y|X] and still tell you nothing useful about E[Y′(t)|X].

By sensitivity

Steep and flat lines: the partition separates responses.

By predicted sales

Parallel lines at different levels: the partition separates sales.

The model's job

Predictive performance is measured on the outcome. If the decision depends on a derivative, a model that predicts the outcome well may still be the wrong tool.

the score

Test R² 0.9124 is high. The model knows which days are busy; it does not know which days are price-sensitive.

the lesson

Choose the target to match the decision. If you will set a price, predict the slope, not the level.

04 the limits

What the model cannot see

A ranking,
not a measurement.

Sensitivity is a counterfactual slope. The model predicts it, but the prediction is not directly observable.

Some predictions are positive: the model says sales would rise with price on those days. That is economically implausible and likely extrapolation. For partitioning, it is enough that the model ranks days by responsiveness; the exact level is not identified.

The next chapter asks the harder question: how do we know whether one CATE model is better than another? A good partition is one that changes the decision usefully, not one that predicts sensitivity most accurately.

Do not over-read the slope

The band slopes are descriptive. The causal claim rests on randomized prices and the model's functional form, not on the test-set regression alone.

The honest sentence

“The model ranks days by predicted price sensitivity. Days in the low-sensitivity band show a steeper price-sales slope in the test set. That is a useful partition, not a calibrated individual effect.”

the use

Personalization needs an ordering. You do not need to know each day's exact elasticity to decide where to be cautious with price.

the warning

Positive predicted sensitivity is a red flag for extrapolation. Check the model's behavior at the edges before acting on it.

05 key ideas

Carry these forward

Four things
to keep.

  1. CATE is a function, not a number. It allows the treatment effect to vary with context; that is what makes personalization possible.
  2. Interactions create heterogeneity. A model without treatment×feature interactions predicts the same sensitivity for everyone.
  3. Sensitivity is a derivative. You can approximate it by predicting the outcome at t and t+1 and taking the difference.
  4. Prediction is not sensitivity. A model can predict sales extremely well and still fail to separate responsive from unresponsive days.

The sentence to avoid

“The model predicts a sensitivity of +0.88, so raising the price will increase sales.” The model predicts a slope; the slope may be extrapolation, and the test-set evidence is a partition, not a guarantee.

to remember

Ask what decision the model changes. If the decision is price, the target should be the price slope.

06 practice

Check yourself

Can you
defend it?

Four short questions. Answer first, then open the field note.

01 What is the difference between ATE and CATE?

The ATE is one average effect for the population. The CATE is the average effect conditional on covariates; it allows different units to respond differently.

02 Why do interactions matter for sensitivity?

Without a treatment×feature interaction, the derivative with respect to treatment is a constant. The interaction lets the predicted slope change with the features.

03 Why did the predictive model fail to separate sensitivities?

It was trained to predict the sales level. Its bands split high- and low-sales days, but price slopes were similar across bands; predicting Y is not predicting dY/dT.

04 What should you check before acting on a sensitivity prediction?

Whether the model is extrapolating (for example, positive price sensitivity), whether the partition changes the decision usefully, and whether the causal assumptions hold.
