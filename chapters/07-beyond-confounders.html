Good controls. Bad controls.

not every control helps ↓

A variable on the right-hand side is not automatically a virtue.

Controls that predict the outcome can sharpen an estimate. Controls that predict only the treatment can blur it. Controls that sit after the treatment can erase the effect. The graph tells you which is which.

01 the control diet

Good controls

Predict the outcome,
not just the treatment.

A good control explains variance in the outcome. It makes the signal easier to see, even when it is not a confounder.

In the collections experiment, email was randomised. The naive effect on payments is −0.62 reais, with a standard error of 2.94: indistinguishable from zero. The outcome is noisy because credit limits and risk scores vary enormously.

Add those two pre-treatment predictors and the email coefficient becomes +4.43 with a standard error of 2.13. The estimate is now statistically distinguishable from zero. Nothing about the randomisation changed; the residual variance did.

after credit & risk 5,652
the precision budget Var(β̂) = σ2Σ(Ti − T̄)2

A good control lowers the numerator by shrinking the residual variance. But a control that predicts only the treatment lowers the denominator: it makes the treatment look less variable, so the same effect is harder to estimate. That is the hospital lesson.

The decision rule

Include confounders and strong outcome predictors. Exclude variables that predict only the treatment, and exclude variables caused by the treatment.

the surprise

The naive email effect is negative, but not because email hurts payments. It is because the outcome is so noisy that the estimate is uninformative.

the limit

Good controls improve precision. They do not fix confounding; that is a separate job for the graph.

02 email bench

Same experiment, different controls

Good controls
versus bad controls.

Three models on the same 5,000 customers. The only difference is what is held fixed.

Credit limit and risk score are pre-treatment predictors of payments; they belong. Opened and agreement are post-treatment: they are consequences of the email and part of the channel through which it works. Holding them fixed asks a different question — and the email coefficient collapses to −1.61.

try it

Start with no controls, then add credit and risk. The coefficient flips sign. Then add opened and agreement: it collapses again.

the rule

Pre-treatment predictors of the outcome: yes. Post-treatment consequences: no. The clock matters as much as the correlation.

03 hospital bench

Mostly harmful controls

A control that
blurs the signal.

Severity is a confounder. Hospital predicts treatment, but not days once severity is in the model. Adding it raises the standard error.

Two hospitals run randomised trials with different treatment probabilities, and the hospital with more severe cases gives the drug more often. Without severity, the drug looks harmful: +14.15 days. Control for severity and the estimate becomes −7.59 days.

Should hospital also be a control? It predicts treatment, not the outcome once severity is held fixed. Adding it moves the estimate to −5.09 and raises the standard error from 2.27 to 3.49. It consumes treatment variation without explaining days.

the variance

Adding hospital cuts the residual variance of treatment from 0.234 to 0.058. Less treatment variation means a larger standard error.

the trade-off

A control that predicts only treatment is not a bias fix. It is a precision tax.

04 selection bias

Bad controls

Do not control
for the funnel.

Opened and agreement are caused by the email. They are the funnel through which the email works, not confounders to hold fixed.

Email → opened → agreement → payments. Conditioning on a mediator removes the part of the effect that runs through it. Conditioning on a common effect can open a path that was closed. Both are selection bias, and both can happen even in a randomised experiment.

The result is the collapse from +4.43 to −1.61 in the email model. The bad model has lower residual variance, which makes it look more precise. Precision is not identification.

The Bad COP

Do not split an outcome into “did anything happen” and “how much happened, given that it did.” Conditioning on participation selects on a consequence of treatment. The participation model can be fine; the conditional-on-positives effect is biased even under randomisation.

the tempting decomposition E[Y | T] = E[Y | Y > 0, T] · P(Y > 0 | T)

The identity is true. Estimating the second factor on the subset with positive outcomes is not. The treated and control groups are no longer comparable: the control group loses the people who would have spent only under treatment. That is selection on the outcome’s participation margin.

The four-way rule

Include confounders. Include outcome predictors. Exclude treatment-only predictors. Exclude mediators and colliders. When in doubt, draw the graph before you add the column.

the test

Ask: could this variable have been measured before treatment? If not, it is a consequence. Consequences are not controls.

the trap

“But it improves the R²” is not an argument. A mediator can improve fit and bias the coefficient at the same time.

05 key ideas

Carry these forward

Four things
to keep.

  1. Outcome predictors are precision. A control that explains Y can shrink the residual variance and sharpen the estimate, even if it is not a confounder.
  2. Treatment-only predictors are a tax. They eat the variation in T without explaining Y; the standard error grows.
  3. Post-treatment controls are dangerous. Mediators and colliders can remove the effect or open a non-causal path, even under randomisation.
  4. Precision is not identification. A smaller standard error around a biased coefficient is still a biased coefficient.

The kitchen sink

More controls can mean less variance, more variance, or a new bias. The graph decides which column belongs; the data only decides how precisely it is estimated.

to remember

Before adding a control, ask: does it predict Y, T, both, or neither — and was it measured before treatment?

06 practice

Check yourself

Can you
defend it?

Four short questions. Answer first, then open the field note.

01 Why can a control that is not a confounder still be useful?

If it predicts the outcome, it reduces the residual variance. A smaller numerator in the variance formula means a smaller standard error and a sharper estimate, even though the control does not change the identification argument.

02 Why does adding hospital increase the standard error?

Hospital predicts treatment but not days once severity is controlled. It absorbs treatment variation, reducing the denominator of the variance formula. Less treatment variation means a larger standard error.

03 Why are opened and agreement bad controls?

They are caused by the email and lie on the causal path to payments. Holding them fixed removes part of the effect of the email; they are mediators, not confounders.

04 Why is the Bad COP biased even under randomisation?

Conditioning on a positive outcome selects on a consequence of treatment. The control group loses the people who would have spent only under treatment, so the treated and control groups are no longer comparable.
