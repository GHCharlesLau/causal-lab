The most dangerous equation.

n is in the denominator ↓

A difference of 4.91 points is an estimate. How much of it is noise?

The standard error, the confidence interval, and the p-value are three ways of saying how sure we are. They are also the easiest things in this book to misuse.

01 the standard error

The error bar is not optional

An estimate
has a spread.

A sample mean is one draw from a distribution of possible sample means. Its standard deviation has a name: the standard error.

The online group averages 73.64. If we reran the experiment with a new group of 94 students, we would not get exactly 73.64 again. The standard error answers: how much would the average move from sample to sample?

For a sample mean, the standard error is the sample standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size:

the equation SE = s√n

The square root is what makes the equation dangerous. To halve the error, you need four times the data. Small samples do not just give noisy averages; they make extreme averages common.

In the online-classroom data, the standard error is 1.54 for online and 0.87 for face-to-face. The online group is smaller and more variable, so its mean is less precise.

Online

1.54 s = 14.90 · n = 94

Face-to-face

0.87 s = 9.56 · n = 120

The gap

1.77 SE of the difference, √(s12/n1 + s02/n0)

What it is not

The standard error is not the standard deviation of the data. The data are spread around the mean; the standard error is the spread of the mean itself around the truth.

the dangerous part

Halve the sample, and the error grows by √2 ≈ 1.41. Quarter it, and the error doubles. Small samples are expensive in a way that is easy to miss.

sanity check

The online group is smaller (94 vs. 120) and has a larger standard deviation (14.9 vs. 9.6). Both push its standard error up.

02 the sample-size dial

The most dangerous equation, in motion

More data.
Less noise.

A population with true mean 74 and standard deviation 2. Draw a sample, average it, repeat 400 times. Change n and watch the distribution of averages.

This is the book’s simulation, made visible. At n = 10, one experiment can land far from 74. At n = 500, the averages pile up tightly around the truth. The true mean never moves; only the precision changes.

The mistake the equation invites

A small school can top the league table by luck. A small treatment group can produce a dramatic effect by luck. Ranking by an estimate without its standard error is a machine for generating false stories.

try it

Set n = 10, then n = 1000. Which readout changes faster: the true mean or the standard error?

the fine print

This simulation knows the truth. In real data, the true mean is the thing we are trying to learn.

03 confidence intervals

A range, not a guess

The gap
has an interval.

A 95% confidence interval is a procedure that captures the truth in 95% of repeated experiments. It is not a 95% probability that this particular interval contains the truth.

The online-minus-face-to-face gap is −4.91 points. Its standard error is 1.77. A 95% interval uses 1.96 standard errors:

the interval estimate ± z × SE    ⇒    −4.91 ± 1.96 × 1.77

That gives (−8.38, −1.45). The interval excludes zero, so at the 95% level we reject “no difference.” But the interval is wide: the data are compatible with a 1.45-point loss and an 8.38-point loss.

The sentence to avoid

“There is a 95% chance the true effect is in this interval.” The true effect is fixed; the interval is random. The procedure has 95% coverage, not this one interval.

coverage

If we repeated the experiment many times and built an interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the truth.

the trade

99% intervals are wider. That is the price of more confidence, and it is not free.

04 p-values

What a p-value is not

A small p
is not a big effect.

The p-value is the probability of seeing a statistic this extreme if the null hypothesis were true. It is not the probability that the null is true.

The z statistic is the estimate divided by its standard error: z = −4.91 / 1.77 = −2.78. Under the null, z is standard normal. The book reports the one-sided p-value:

the test z = −4.911.77 = −2.78    pone-sided = 0.0027    ptwo-sided = 0.0054

A p-value of 0.0027 is small. It says: if there were no difference, a gap at least this far from zero would be rare. It does not say the effect is large, important, or robust to a different design. The interval is the more informative object.

The book uses the one-sided value because the alternative is directional — online is worse. Report which one you use. A two-sided test doubles it.

Three sentences that are false

“The p-value is the probability the null is true.” “A p < 0.05 proves a real effect.” “A smaller p means a larger effect.” None of them follows from the definition.

The garden of forking paths

If you try many outcomes, subgroups, or model specifications and report only the one with p < 0.05, the p-value no longer means what it says. The design has to be honest about the search.

the definition

p = P(statistic at least as extreme as observed | null is true). The vertical bar is doing all the work.

p is not a verdict

Significance is a threshold convention, not a property of the world. The estimate and its interval are the report.

05 key ideas

Carry these forward

Three things
to keep.

  1. The standard error scales as 1/√n. Precision is expensive: four times the data buys half the error.
  2. Report an interval, not just a point. The interval shows the range of effects compatible with the data and the design.
  3. A p-value is a probability about the data, conditional on a null. It is not the probability that the null is true, nor a measure of importance.

The most dangerous equation

Small samples produce extreme averages. That is why the top of a league table is full of small schools — and why an exciting subgroup result from 12 people deserves a standard error, not a headline.

to remember

An estimate without an error bar is a story with the uncertainty edited out.

06 practice

Check yourself

Can you
defend it?

Four short questions. Answer first, then open the field note.

01 Why does the standard error shrink with the square root of n, not with n?

Because the sample mean averages n independent pieces of noise. Their variance adds, but the variance of the average is the variance of one observation divided by n; the standard deviation — the standard error — therefore scales as 1/√n.

02 The 95% interval is (−8.38, −1.45). What does it rule out, and what does it not rule out?

It rules out zero at the 95% level under the model. It does not rule out a 1.5-point loss or an 8-point loss; those are inside the interval. It also does not rule out bias from a broken design.

03 The book reports p = 0.0027. Why is the two-sided value 0.0054?

The one-sided test asks how often a z at least as negative as −2.78 occurs under the null. The two-sided test also counts equally extreme positive values, so it doubles the one-sided p-value. The book’s alternative is directional, so it reports the one-sided value.

04 Why can a small school top the league table?

Its average is based on few students, so its standard error is large. Extreme averages are more likely when n is small, even if every school has the same true quality. Ranking by the estimate without its uncertainty selects for noise.
