01 frisch-waugh-lovell

Nuisance parameters

Control without
modeling everything.

FWL says you can partial out the confounders first, then estimate the treatment effect from residuals.

Estimate Y on X, estimate T on X, then regress the outcome residual on the treatment residual. For linear models, this gives the exact same treatment coefficient as one big regression.

That matters because the confounders are nuisance: temperature, weekday, and cost are not the target. FWL lets us model them separately, and later replace the linear nuisance models with flexible ML.

The sign flip

Raw price and sales rise together because busy days have both higher demand and higher prices. Once demand is held fixed, price reduces sales.

the intuition

Residuals are the part of each variable that the confounders cannot explain. Regressing those parts isolates the treatment variation.

the catch

FWL is exact only when the nuisance models are correctly specified. Misspecify them and the treatment estimate inherits the bias.

02 cross-fitting

FWL on steroids

Let ML model
the nuisance.

Use flexible models for the outcome and treatment residuals, but make the predictions out of fold.

The treatment model is a “debias” model: it predicts price from temperature, weekday, and cost. The outcome model is a “denoise” model: it predicts sales from the same features. Their residuals are orthogonal to the confounders.

Cross-fitting is essential. If the nuisance model overfits, the residuals shrink and the final regression is biased toward zero. Five-fold out-of-fold predictions keep the residuals honest.

cross-fitting

Each residual comes from a model that never saw that row. That is what stops overfitting from eating the treatment variation.

the result

Orthogonal ATE is −3.92, close to the OLS −4.00. The small gap is the cost of using flexible nuisance models.

03 r-learner

From ATE to CATE

Weight the
residual regression.

Interact the residualized treatment with context for a linear CATE, or fit a weighted ML model for a flexible one.

The residual model Ỹ = τ(X)T̃ + e defines a causal loss. The R-learner rewrites it as a weighted regression: target Ỹ/T̃, weights T̃2. Any predictive model can fit that target.

The parametric version interacts the residual with temperature, weekday, and cost. The nonparametric version lets a gradient-boosted model learn the shape. On this test set they perform similarly.

the target

Ỹ/T̃ is a noisy slope per unit. The weight T̃2 gives more influence to rows with more treatment variation.

the lesson

The R-learner is a framework, not a single model. The final learner can be linear or flexible; the orthogonalization is the constant.

04 the limits

A local slope, not a curve

It is still
a linear approximation.

Double ML assumes the residualized outcome is linear in the residualized treatment. A flexible final model finds the local slope, not the whole response curve.

If the treatment effect is nonlinear, the R-learner can recover how the slope changes with covariates, but it still approximates the effect locally. Extrapolating to a much higher treatment level is risky.

The book calls the fully counterfactual version “non-scientific”: it predicts outcomes at treatment levels far from the observed data. Use it with caution, and never without a holdout check.

Do not extrapolate blindly

A CATE of −2 at the current price does not mean the next 10 reais of price increase will reduce sales by 20. The slope can change with the treatment level.

The honest sentence

“Orthogonal ML removes confounding from the nuisance relationships and estimates a residual treatment effect. It does not turn a local slope into a global causal curve.”

the use

Use orthogonal ML for a credible ATE or a ranking of local sensitivity. Do not use it to promise the outcome at an untested treatment level.

the check

If a counterfactual prediction is far outside the observed treatment range, treat it as a model artifact until validated.

05 key ideas

Carry these forward

Four things
to keep.

  1. FWL separates nuisance from treatment. Residualize the outcome and treatment on confounders, then regress the residuals on each other.
  2. Cross-fitting protects the residuals. Out-of-fold predictions stop overfitting from shrinking the treatment variation.
  3. Double ML is orthogonal. Small errors in the nuisance models do not linearly contaminate the treatment effect.
  4. The R-learner is a causal loss. Target Ỹ/T̃ with weights T̃2; the final model can be linear or flexible.

The sentence to avoid

“Orthogonal ML finds the true nonlinear treatment effect.” It finds a local linear approximation; extrapolation beyond the observed treatment range is not guaranteed.

to remember

Partial out the confounders, cross-fit the nuisance, and estimate the treatment from residuals. The sign flip is the payoff.

06 practice

Check yourself

Can you
defend it?

Four short questions. Answer first, then open the field note.

01 What does FWL say in one sentence?

Regressing Y on X, T on X, and then the Y residual on the T residual gives the same treatment coefficient as regressing Y on X and T together, for linear models.

02 Why is cross-fitting necessary with ML nuisance models?

In-sample predictions overfit, shrinking residuals and biasing the treatment effect toward zero. Out-of-fold predictions keep the residuals honest.

03 Why does the raw price-sales slope have the wrong sign?

Busy days have both higher demand and higher prices. The raw correlation mixes the demand effect with the price effect; controlling for confounders flips it.

04 What does the R-learner minimize?

The squared residual outcome minus the CATE times the residual treatment. Rewritten with weights, it is a weighted regression of Ỹ/T̃ on X.
