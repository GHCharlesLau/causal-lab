00 the question

The hook

The drug looks harmful.
Is it?

20,000 patients. Some get the drug, some don’t. Taken at face value, the drug is a disaster.

Patients on the drug take 16.9 more days to recover. If this were a randomized trial, that number would be the answer. It is not a randomized trial.

Sicker patients are more likely to receive the drug. Severity raises recovery time and the chance of treatment. The naive comparison is mixing the drug’s effect with the reason it was given — the classic confounding problem from the earlier chapters.

A crude fix: compare patients with similar severity. Drag the band below and watch what happens to the estimate.

Key idea

Matching is the discipline of comparing units that are alike on the variables that made treatment non-random.

Common trap

A narrow band is not a free lunch. You lose sample size, and you still have to match on the other confounders.

the target ATE = ∫ ( E[Y | X, T=1] − E[Y | X, T=0] ) dP(x)

Source: Chapter 10 · Matching, Facure. Data: the book’s simulated medicine table.

predict first

Will narrowing the band make the drug look better or worse?

the catch

We are holding severity roughly constant. Age and sex are still free to differ.

01 regression

What regression is doing

Regression is a weighted average in disguise.

Six men and four women. The drug works better on men, but men get almost all of it. Watch how three estimators combine the same two cells.

Men stay longer in hospital and are much more likely to get the drug. Women recover faster and are split evenly. The true effect is −3 days for men and −2 days for women, so the true ATE is −2.6.

The naive comparison says −1.19: too small, because it compares mostly men on the drug with the one man off it. Subclassification compares within each sex and weights by cell size: −2.6, the right answer.

Regression also partitions by sex, but it combines the cells with weights proportional to Nk × Var(Tk). Men have only one control, so their cell gets less weight. The estimate leans toward the women’s −2: −2.45.

Key idea

Regression is a particular weighted average of within-cell comparisons, and the weights depend on the variance of treatment inside each cell.

Common trap

With continuous covariates, regression is still linear and still extrapolates. It is not doing exact cell comparisons.

the weights wk ∝ Nk · Var(Tk)

try it

Switch to OLS. Which cell gains weight? Why does the estimate move toward the women’s effect?

hold on

We only have two cells here. Real datasets have many — and most of them will be empty.

02 cells

Subclassification

Compare like with like, one cell at a time.

If treatment is independent of potential outcomes within a cell of X, the cell-level difference is a clean local effect. Weight the cells and you have the ATE.

The cell idea is the cleanest in causal inference. Partition the data by the confounders, compare treated and control inside each cell, then average the cell effects using the cell’s share of the sample.

With sex alone there are two cells and both are full. Add severity bins and the grid starts to thin. Some cells have treated patients but no controls; some have controls but no treated. The estimator is undefined there.

subclassification ATE = ∑k (Ȳk1 − Ȳk0) · NkN

Key idea

Subclassification is the cleanest idea in matching, and the first to break when X gets big.

Common trap

Empty cells are not a nuisance. They are the data telling you that common support is failing.

try it

Switch from 4 to 8 severity bins. How many cells lose one of the groups?

dimension check

10 covariates × 3 bins each = 310 = 59,049 cells. That is the next section.

03 exact twins

When a twin exists

Find the untreated twin.

Nineteen trainees, twenty-one controls. The trainees are younger. Match each trainee to a control of exactly the same age, and the comparison flips.

At face value, trainees earn $4,297 less than non-trainees. But look at the ages: the trainee group is much younger. Age is a confounder, and it is easy to condition on because the data has exact overlaps.

Take unit 1: age 28, earnings $17,700. Unit 27 is also 28, and earns $8,800. Same age, different life. Compare the two, and the trainee looks better by $8,900. Do this for every trainee and average the within-pair differences.

paired ATET ATET = 1N1 ∑i (Yi − Yj(i))

Key idea

If a credible twin exists, matching is just a paired comparison. The hard part is finding the twin.

Common trap

Exact matching needs exact overlap. Real data has many covariates and no exact twins, so we need a distance.

predict first

Will matching make the training program look better or worse?

look closely

Control units are reused: unit 37 is the match for both unit 3 and unit 5. Matching allows reuse unless you forbid it.

04 nearest neighbour

The main lab

No twin? Find the nearest neighbour.

Three confounders, 20,000 patients, no exact twins. Standardize the features, choose a distance, and let every treated patient borrow the closest control.

The medicine data has three covariates: severity, age, and sex. Distances in raw units are meaningless — age spans decades while severity spans 0 to 1 — so we standardize first. Then each patient is matched to the nearest patient in the opposite group.

The naive comparison says +16.9. One-nearest-neighbour matching says −1.0: the sign is fixed, but the magnitude is far from the truth. The match is not a twin. Correcting for the match discrepancy gets us to −7.36. The simulation truth is −10.

matching ATE ATE = 1N ∑i (2Ti − 1) (Yi − Yjm(i))

Key idea

Matching is a design choice. Distance, scaling, k, replacement, caliper, and correction all change the number.

Common trap

More neighbours is not automatically better. In this data, k=10 moves the uncorrected estimate back toward zero.

predict first

Will more neighbours make the raw estimate closer to −10, or farther away?

real practice

Always report k, the distance metric, the caliper, the share of units dropped, and the correction model.

05 the bias

Matching bias

A match is not a twin.

The estimator compares Yi with the outcome of a similar — not identical — unit. The difference between their expected untreated outcomes is bias.

Write μ0(x) for the expected outcome of an untreated unit with covariates x. A treated unit i and its match j are close, but xi ≠ xj. The gap μ0(xi) − μ0(xj) leaks into the comparison.

For the first patient in the medicine data, the raw outcome is 31 and the matched control’s is 39. A regression fitted on the untreated predicts a μ0 gap of about 4.4 days. Subtract that estimated gap from the pair, and the comparison gets closer to the truth. Across all pairs, the correction moves the estimate from −1.00 to −7.36.

The regression is not extrapolating over treatment. It is a local correction for the fact that the match is imperfect. The meat of the estimator is still the match.

bias-corrected ATET = 1N1 ∑i [ (Yi − Yj(i)) − (μ0(Xi) − μ0(Xj(i))) ]

Key idea

Bias correction does not replace matching; it pays for the fact that the match is imperfect.

Common trap

A linear correction can only remove the part of the discrepancy a line can see. Nonlinearity leaves residual bias.

compare

Toggle bias correct in the lab above. The estimate jumps by more than six days.

not a free lunch

The correction model is an assumption. If it is wrong, the correction can make things worse.

06 the curse

Dimensionality

Every covariate makes the twin harder to find.

Matching on one variable is easy. On ten, the feature space is mostly empty: the nearest neighbour gets farther and farther away.

In one dimension, 500 points give a nearest neighbour at a typical distance of a few hundredths. Add dimensions and the same 500 points spread out. The nearest neighbour distance rises, and with it the matching discrepancy.

Subclassification feels the same curse from the other side. Split 10 covariates into 3 bins each and you need 310 = 59,049 cells. Most will be empty.

This is why the next chapter compresses the covariates into a single dimension: the propensity score. It is also why regression, which projects X onto Y, handles high dimensions more gracefully than raw matching.

Key idea

The curse is not a bug in matching; it is the price of asking for a twin in a high-dimensional space.

Common trap

Adding a covariate can reduce confounding bias and increase matching bias at the same time. The net effect is empirical.

Next chapter

Propensity Score

If matching gets harder with every covariate, compress the covariates into one number: the probability of treatment. That is the propensity score — and it is the next chapter.

The 12-second primer

Same age. Different lives.

The film is still being rendered.

The interactive chapter is the main event. The HyperFrames composition lives in motion/index.html; render it with the HyperFrames CLI, or just use the lab.

Transcript

Nineteen trainees. Twenty-one controls. The naive gap is four thousand, two hundred and ninety-seven dollars against the trainees. But the trainees are younger. Match each trainee to a control of the same age, and the gap becomes two thousand, four hundred and fifty-eight dollars in their favour. Same age. Different lives. That is matching.

Provenance

Where the numbers come from

Trainees. The 40-row table used in the chapter: 19 trainees, 21 controls, age and earnings. It is contrived to make exact matching visible.

Medicine. 20,000 patients simulated in the book’s companion notebook. Columns: sex, age, severity, medication, recovery. 6,924 are treated.

The truth line. The simulation sets recovery as a deterministic function of the covariates minus ten times medication, so the true ATE is exactly −10 days. The book does not reveal this; it is included here so you can see estimator bias, not just confounding bias. The oracle model in the lab uses the simulation’s functional form and recovers about −10.05; it is not a practical estimator.

What we reproduce. The book’s naive +16.90, 1-NN −0.9954, and bias-corrected −7.3627. Small differences can come from tie-breaking.

Plain words

Glossary

Counterfactual
The outcome a unit would have had under the treatment it did not receive.
Confounder
A variable that affects both treatment and outcome, creating a backdoor path.
ATE
Average treatment effect across the whole population.
ATET
Average treatment effect on the treated.
Common support
The region of covariate space where both treated and control units exist.
Caliper
A maximum allowed match distance; farther matches are dropped.
Bias correction
Subtracting an estimate of the outcome gap caused by imperfect matching.
