The model with the better R² is the worse causal model. The metric is measuring the wrong thing.

01 the simulation

Two kinds of features

Nuisance
and effect.

The outcome depends on 20 nuisance features that do not modify the treatment effect, and 10 features that do.

Treatment is randomized. The outcome is T + T·Wγ + 20X·ν + noise. The X features drive the level; the W features drive the heterogeneity.

M1 regresses the outcome on the nuisance X features and their interactions with treatment. M2 uses the effect W features and their interactions. Both are correct linear models of their own subset; the difference is which variation they capture.

The setup

Random treatment removes the identification problem. The only question left is whether the model is measuring the level or the slope.

the models

Both are linear regressions with treatment interactions. M1 sees the nuisance features; M2 sees the effect features.

the trap

If the metric is R², M1 looks excellent and M2 looks broken. If the goal is a policy, the ranking flips.

02 the scoreboard

R² vs cumulative elasticity

A great forecast,
a flat effect.

M1 explains 91.6% of the test variance. M2 explains 8.4%. On the causal ranking, M2 wins.

M1 · nuisance0.9162.92

M1’s cumulative elasticity is close to flat because its predictions are driven by the nuisance features. M2’s curve is steeper: it separates the customers whose treatment effect is large from those whose effect is small.

The two curves end at the same average elasticity. That is the point: R² measures the path to the endpoint, not the shape of the ranking.

Do not report R² as a causal metric

R² asks how well the model predicts the level. A causal model is judged by how well it ranks the slope. Those can point in opposite directions.

the test

50,000 held-out rows, random treatment. The models are fit on the full simulated data, as in the book.

the warning

A low R² is not evidence of a bad causal model, and a high R² is not evidence of a good one.

03 the curve bench

Cumulative elasticity

Rank the slope,
not the level.

Sort the test rows by each model’s CATE prediction, then estimate the treatment slope in the top share.

the curve

Each point is the treatment slope among the top k customers, multiplied by k/N. The endpoint is the overall slope.

the lesson

The curve rewards the model that puts high-effect customers at the top. R² rewards the model that predicts the level.

04 residualized R²

Remove the nuisance first

Both scores
go negative.

Fit a denoising model on the features, residualize the outcome, and score the causal models again.

After removing the nuisance variation, M1’s R² collapses to −281.8 and M2’s to −24.0. Both are terrible as level predictions, because the level was already explained. But M2 is less terrible, and it is the one with the better elasticity curve.

The residualized score is a diagnostic, not a policy metric. It shows that the original R² was mostly measuring nuisance, not effect.

Negative R² is allowed

On a residualized target, R² can be arbitrarily negative. It is not a probability and not a percentage of explained variance in the usual sense.

the denoiser

A linear model of Y on all 30 features, fit on the training half. The residual removes the part of Y that the features can predict.

the interpretation

Residualized R² compares models on the part of the outcome that is not nuisance. It is a sharper diagnostic, not a validation metric.

05 key ideas

Carry these forward

Four things
to keep.

  1. R² measures the level. A model can explain 92% of the variance through nuisance features and know nothing about the effect.
  2. Cumulative elasticity measures the ranking. It rewards the model that puts high-effect units at the top, regardless of its R².
  3. Residualize to diagnose. Removing the nuisance variation exposes how much of the R² was effect versus level.
  4. Choose the metric to match the decision. If the decision is a treatment assignment, the metric must be about the treatment effect.

The sentence to avoid

“M1 has a higher R², so it is the better causal model.” M1’s R² comes from the nuisance features; its treatment-effect ranking is nearly flat.

to remember

Report the curve, not just the score. The scoreboard should look like the decision.

06 practice

Check yourself

Can you
defend it?

Four short questions. Answer first, then open the field note.

01 Why does M1 have a high R²?

It uses the 20 nuisance features that drive the level of the outcome. Their interactions with treatment absorb most of the variance, even though they do not drive the effect.

02 Why does M2 rank the effect better?

It uses the features that actually modify the treatment effect. Its CATE predictions sort the high-effect units near the top, so the cumulative elasticity curve rises faster.

03 Why are the residualized R² values negative?

The residual target has most of its variance removed. A level model can be worse than predicting the mean, which gives a negative R².

04 Which metric should drive a policy decision?

The cumulative elasticity or gain curve, because it measures the ranking of treatment effects. R² measures the level and can favor the wrong model.
