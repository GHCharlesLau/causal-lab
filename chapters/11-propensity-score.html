01 the psychology

The psychology of growth

The schools
are not the same.

A growth-mindset program is assigned to some schools. The schools that adopt it differ in poverty, achievement, and expectations — so the raw comparison is not the effect.

The naive difference is +0.4723 achievement points, with a standard error of 0.020. It is large, precise, and confounded. The students who receive the intervention are not a random sample of the 10,391 students in the data.

The treatment rate rises with success_expect, from about 27% in the lowest category to 36% in the highest. That is evidence of selection on a covariate, not proof of the effect.

the score e(X) = P(T = 1 | X)

The propensity score is not the causal effect. It is the probability of treatment given the covariates — a one-number summary of the assignment mechanism. If two units have the same score, their covariate distributions are balanced in expectation.

The balancing property

Conditioning on the propensity score balances every covariate that went into it. That is why weighting and matching on the score can mimic a randomized comparison — if the score is right and the overlap is there.

the check

Before weighting, look at the score distribution in both groups. If the treated and control scores barely overlap, no weight can fix the comparison.

the limit

The score only balances what you measured. Unmeasured confounding survives the best propensity model.

02 the score bench

Estimation and overlap

Do the groups
share a score?

Fit a logistic model for treatment. Plot the scores for treated and control. Positivity is a property of the data, not of the estimator.

The book estimates the score with a logistic regression on school and student covariates. The estimated scores range from about 0.13 to 0.53; both groups occupy the same region. That is the overlap that makes weighting possible.

the shape

The scores cluster tightly. That is common when treatment is assigned at the school level and covariates are standardized.

the tail

A few scores near zero would get enormous weights. Always check the distribution before trusting IPTW.

03 weighting

Inverse probability weighting

Give the rare
types more weight.

IPTW builds a pseudo-population in which treatment is independent of the covariates. The treated mean is weighted by 1/e(X); the control mean by 1/(1−e(X)).

The weighted treated mean is 0.2596; the weighted control mean is −0.1289. Their difference is the IPTW ATE, 0.3885. The raw difference was 0.4723; weighting removes about 0.084 of the naive gap.

A 1,000-sample bootstrap gives an ATE of 0.3877 with a 95% interval of (0.3545, 0.4199). The estimate is stable across resamples and comfortably above zero.

the weights

The treated pseudo-population sums to 10,388.6; the control pseudo-population to 10,391.4. Both are close to the original 10,391.

the caveat

IPTW is only as good as the score model. A misspecified logistic regression reweights the wrong population.

04 matching

Matching on the score

One score,
one neighbor.

Instead of matching on 29 covariates, match on the probability. The book’s nearest-neighbor PS match gives ATE 0.389, ATT 0.405, ATC 0.382.

Matching and weighting answer slightly different questions. IPTW targets the ATE by reweighting the whole sample. Nearest-neighbor matching on the score compares each unit with its closest counterpart; the book reports ATE, ATT, and ATC separately.

The regression adjustment that includes the score linearly gives 0.3930. The three adjusted estimates — 0.3885, 0.389, 0.3930 — are close. The naive 0.4723 is the outlier.

Common issues

Positivity: if scores do not overlap, the counterfactual is missing. Model misspecification: the score is estimated, not known. Extreme weights: scores near 0 or 1 explode. Unmeasured confounding: the score balances only what is in it.

The honest sentence

“After weighting on the estimated propensity score, the intervention is associated with a 0.39-point achievement gain — if the score model captured the relevant confounders and the groups overlap.”

the agreement

Weighting, regression, and matching all land near 0.39. That is a good sign for the specification, not a guarantee against unmeasured confounding.

the question

Which estimand do you need? ATE for the population, ATT for the treated, ATC for the controls. Matching can report all three; IPTW is usually targeted at the ATE.

05 key ideas

Carry these forward

Four things
to keep.

  1. The score is a probability, not an effect. It summarizes the assignment mechanism; the causal estimate comes from weighting or matching with it.
  2. Balance is the point. Conditioning on the score balances the covariates that entered it. Check balance before interpreting the outcome.
  3. Positivity is non-negotiable. If treated and control scores do not overlap, there is no comparable counterfactual to weight toward.
  4. Unmeasured confounding survives. A propensity score can only balance what you measured; it cannot fix what you did not.

The kitchen sink, again

Adding more covariates can improve balance, but it can also create extreme scores and violate overlap. The score model is a design choice, not a data dump.

to remember

Plot the score distribution before you weight. Plot the balance after you weight. Then, and only then, read the outcome.

06 practice

Check yourself

Can you
defend it?

Four short questions. Answer first, then open the field note.

01 What is a propensity score?

The probability of receiving treatment given the observed covariates: e(X) = P(T=1|X). It is a balancing score, not a causal effect.

02 How does IPTW use the score?

It weights treated units by 1/e(X) and controls by 1/(1−e(X)). In the weighted pseudo-population, treatment is independent of the covariates, so the weighted outcome difference estimates the ATE.

03 Why is overlap important?

If some treated units have scores where no controls exist, there is no comparable counterfactual. Weighting would rely on extrapolation and extreme weights.

04 What can the propensity score not fix?

Unmeasured confounding. The score balances only the covariates included in the model; an omitted confounder still biases the weighted or matched comparison.
