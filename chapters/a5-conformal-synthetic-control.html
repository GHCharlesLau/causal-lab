01 synthetic control

One treated, many controls

Build the
counterfactual.

Use the untreated states to construct a synthetic California that matches its pre-1988 cigarette sales.

Synthetic control fits non-negative weights on the control states so their weighted sum tracks California before Proposition 99. After 1988, the gap between California and its synthetic twin is the estimated effect.

The estimate is a path, not a single number: the gap is about −1.87 in 1988 and −26.60 by 2000. A path needs a path-valued inference procedure.

The inferential problem

There is one treated unit. The usual standard error is not available. The placebo test compares California’s gap with the gaps of untreated states, but it assumes random assignment of the intervention.

the fit

The SC weights minimize squared pre-treatment error, sum to one, and are non-negative. The fit is good enough that the pre-period gap is small.

the weakness

A single post-period number hides the path. Inference should respect the dependence across years.

02 conformal residuals

Test a null trajectory

Shift the data,
refit, compare.

Under a null, subtract it from the post-period outcomes, refit the SC on all years, and measure the post-period residuals.

For H0: no effect, the counterfactual equals the observed California path. Fit the SC on the whole null series and compute residuals. Large negative post-period residuals are evidence against the null.

The test statistic is the mean absolute post-period residual: 12.60. To calibrate it, block-permute the residual series and recompute the statistic on the post-period block. The p-value is the share of permutations at least as extreme: 0.161.

p-value p = (1/T) ∑k 1{S(u(k)) ≥ S(u)}

Not significant at 10%

The p-value for the no-effect null is 0.161. At α = 0.1, we do not reject the hypothesis that Proposition 99 had no effect on the whole trajectory — even though the point estimates look large.

the statistic

The book uses q = 1: the mean absolute residual in the post period. The mean, not the sum, because the window length varies across tests.

the block

Rolling the residual series preserves its dependence. Permuting individual years would break the serial correlation and overstate significance.

03 the interval bench

Year by year

The band
widens.

Invert the test for a grid of null effects at each post-period year. The 90% interval is the set of nulls that survive.

the inversion

For each candidate null, compute the conformal p-value. Keep the nulls with p ≥ 0.1; their range is the 90% interval.

the reading

In 1988 the data are compatible with no effect. By 1991 they are not. The interval is a statement about each year, not a simultaneous band.

04 reading the result

What the interval says

A slow
policy.

The effect is not distinguishable from zero in the first years. It becomes clearly negative later, and larger in magnitude.

YearEffect90% CI

This is more honest than a single “significant” star. A policy can take years to change behavior, and the interval shows when the data start to rule out no effect.

The conformal guarantee is finite-sample and distribution-free, but it is conditional on the SC model being the right counterfactual model. It does not fix a bad pre-treatment fit.

The correct sentence

“The 90% interval excludes zero from 1991 onward; the 1988 interval includes zero.” That is a statement about each year, supported by the conformal inversion.

the assumption

The procedure assumes exchangeability of the residual blocks, which is plausible if the SC model is stable and the errors are stationary.

the limit

Conformal inference does not rescue a counterfactual model that misses a structural break. It prices the uncertainty; it does not remove the bias.

05 key ideas

Carry these forward

Four things
to keep.

  1. Refit under the null. Subtract the hypothesized effect from the post period, fit the SC on the whole null series, and keep the residuals.
  2. Block-permute, not unit-permute. Rolling the residual series preserves serial dependence and calibrates the test statistic.
  3. Invert the test for an interval. Keep the nulls with p ≥ α; their range is the confidence interval for that year.
  4. The interval changes the story. The 1988 interval contains zero; the 2000 interval does not. Report the path, not just the endpoint.

The sentence to avoid

“The SC effect is −26.6, so Proposition 99 worked.” The effect is a path with uncertainty; in 1988 the data cannot distinguish it from zero.

to remember

Conformal inference gives a p-value and an interval without a parametric likelihood. The price is the exchangeability assumption.

06 practice

Check yourself

Can you
defend it?

Four short questions. Answer first, then open the field note.

01 Why refit the SC under the null?

The null determines the counterfactual series. Refitting under that series gives residuals that are comparable across the null grid, avoiding huge post-period residuals from a pre-fit model.

02 Why block-permute residuals?

Residuals are serially correlated. Permuting individual years breaks that dependence; rolling the whole series preserves it and calibrates the statistic correctly.

03 How is the confidence interval built?

For each candidate null effect, compute the conformal p-value. The interval is the set of nulls that are not rejected at the chosen alpha.

04 What does the 1988 interval mean?

The data are compatible with effects from −12.3 to 8.7, including zero. We cannot rule out no effect in the first year.
