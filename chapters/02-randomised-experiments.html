01 the gold standard

Why a coin is different

Break the link
before treatment.

Randomisation does not make the treated and control identical. It makes their potential outcomes independent of treatment — and that is enough for the difference in means.

In the last chapter, the bias term was the difference in untreated outcomes between the groups. A coin removes that difference in expectation: the treated and untreated are drawn from the same pool, so their potential outcomes have the same distribution.

Formally, random assignment makes the pair of potential outcomes independent of treatment:

the assumption (Y0, Y1) ⊥ T

This does not say the observed outcome is independent of treatment. We hope treatment changes the outcome. It says the outcomes the units would have had do not predict who gets treated. In particular, the untreated outcomes of the treated and control groups have the same mean:

the result E[Y0 | T=1] = E[Y0 | T=0]  ⇒  E[Y | T=1] − E[Y | T=0] = ATE

The whole trick

Randomisation moves the selection problem into the design. You do not have to model why a student chose online classes; you made that choice unrelated to the student.

In expectation, not every sample

A coin can still produce an unlucky split. The estimate is unbiased in expectation, but any one experiment can be imbalanced. That is why the next chapter is about uncertainty.

one sentence

Randomisation makes the treatment independent of the potential outcomes, not of the observed outcome.

not a magic wand

Random assignment fixes selection. It does not fix a broken implementation, attrition, or a treatment that people refuse to take.

02 in a school far away

The online-classroom experiment

One exam.
Three formats.

323 students were randomly assigned to face-to-face, blended, or online classes. At the end of the semester, everyone took the same exam.

This is the kind of comparison the previous chapter could not make. Students did not choose their format. The assignment mechanism did. The groups differ on the exam by 4.91 points between online and face-to-face, and by 1.45 points between blended and face-to-face.

The estimate

The ATE of online versus face-to-face is −4.91 exam points. The blended format is −1.45. These are simple differences in means — and the design is what makes them credible.

try it first

Before clicking: which format do you expect to have the widest spread? Then look at the dots, not just the mean.

the fine print

One zero score sits at the bottom of the data. It is real, and it is included in the means. Do not quietly drop it to make the chart prettier.

03 check the coin

Balance

Did the coin
do its job?

A good sanity check is to compare pre-treatment variables across groups. If randomisation worked, they should look similar — within the noise of a finite sample.

The table below uses the experiment’s pre-treatment variables. Gender looks close. The Black share does not: 2.9% online versus 7.1% face-to-face. The book flags this as a small-sample imbalance, not evidence that the randomisation failed.

Chance is not bias

A finite randomized experiment can be imbalanced. The right response is not to “control for” the imbalance automatically; it is to report it, and to remember that the estimate is unbiased in expectation, not in every sample.

the check

Gender: 63.3% face-to-face, 55.0% blended, 54.3% online. Similar, not identical. Randomisation is a distribution, not a guarantee.

the honest sentence

“The groups look balanced on most covariates; the Black share is lower online, which is consistent with chance in a 323-person sample.”

04 the ideal experiment

When you cannot randomise

The experiment
you cannot run.

RCTs are the cleanest design, but they are often expensive, unethical, or impossible. The next best move is to reason about the assignment mechanism.

You cannot randomly assign pregnant mothers to smoke, or countries to a minimum wage, or customers to a credit line just to see what happens. In those settings, the assignment mechanism is the thing you have to argue about.

That is why the ideal-experiment question is useful even when you cannot run it: if I could randomise anything, what would I randomise, and what would that isolate? The answer tells you which comparison the observational design is trying to approximate.

The data cannot reveal the assignment mechanism by itself. A correlation between education and wealth is consistent with education causing wealth, or with wealthy families buying education. Causal inference is the discipline of making that choice explicit — and then testing the implications.

the question

If you could run the perfect experiment, what would you assign, to whom, and for how long?

the limit

An RCT identifies the effect of the treatment you assigned, for the people who took it. It does not automatically generalise to another population or another version of the treatment.

05 key ideas

Carry these forward

Three things
to keep.

  1. Randomise the assignment, not the outcome. The potential outcomes become independent of treatment; the observed outcome should still depend on it.
  2. The difference in means is the estimate. Under randomisation, no regression is needed to make it causal; the design does the identification.
  3. Balance is a check, not a proof. Finite samples can be unlucky; report the imbalance instead of pretending it away.

Common trap

“It was randomised, so it is significant.” Randomisation removes selection bias; it does not remove sampling noise. The next chapter puts a standard error on the gap.

to remember

Randomisation is a design, not an analysis technique. You cannot add it after the data arrive.

06 practice

Check yourself

Can you
defend it?

Four short questions. Answer first, then open the field note.

01 Why does random assignment make the difference in means causal?

It makes the potential outcomes independent of treatment, so the treated and control groups have the same distribution of untreated outcomes. The bias term E[Y0|T=1] − E[Y0|T=0] is zero in expectation, leaving the ATE.

02 The online group averages 73.64 and face-to-face averages 78.55. What is the estimate, and what exactly is it estimating?

The difference is −4.91 exam points. Under randomisation it estimates the average effect of being assigned to online rather than face-to-face for the students in this experiment. It is not automatically the effect for a different population or a different online course.

03 The Black share is lower online. Does that mean the randomisation failed?

Not necessarily. A 323-person experiment can produce an imbalance by chance. The right response is to report it and to avoid overinterpreting a single covariate. A formal balance test or a larger sample would sharpen the check.

04 Why can’t we always randomise, and what do we do instead?

Randomisation can be unethical, expensive, or infeasible. Then we reason explicitly about the assignment mechanism and use designs — regression, matching, instruments, panels, discontinuities — that try to reconstruct a credible comparison.
