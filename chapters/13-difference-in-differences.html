01 the billboards

Three billboards in the south of Brazil

Both cities
grew.

The campaign ran in Porto Alegre for June. Florianópolis, a nearby capital with no campaign, provides the trend Porto Alegre would have followed.

Deposits in Porto Alegre rose from 46.02 in May to 87.06 in July: a gain of 41.05. That looks like a success. But Florianópolis rose from 171.64 to 206.17, a gain of 34.52, without any billboards.

Subtract the control trend from the treated change and the campaign’s estimated effect is 6.52 reais per customer. The before-and-after number is too optimistic; comparing the cities after the campaign is too pessimistic, because Porto Alegre started at a much lower level.

DiD (YPOA,Jul − YPOA,May) − (YFL,Jul − YFL,May)

The estimator is a difference of two changes. It does not require the cities to have the same baseline, and it does not assume the outcome is flat. It requires the counterfactual change in Porto Alegre to equal the observed change in Florianópolis.

The parallel trends assumption

Without the campaign, Porto Alegre would have grown by the same amount as Florianópolis. That is an assumption about a counterfactual trend, not something the data can prove.

the arithmetic

41.05 − 34.52 = 6.52. The treated change, minus the control change.

the scale

Porto Alegre’s baseline is less than a third of Florianópolis’s. Levels differ enormously; the DiD only uses changes.

The same four means support three very different claims. The choice of comparison is the causal assumption.

The four group means are the whole dataset in miniature. The before-and-after comparison uses only Porto Alegre; the cross-section uses only July; DiD uses all four cells.

why change?

The cities are not comparable in levels. DiD compares their changes, which is a much weaker requirement.

why not just July?

July alone says Porto Alegre is far below Florianópolis. That is a level difference, not a campaign effect.

03 the regression

The interaction term

The same number,
with an error bar.

Run a regression with city, period, and their interaction. The interaction coefficient is the DiD estimate, and it comes with a standard error.

The regression is Y = β0 + β1POA + β2Jul + β3POA×Jul + e. The interaction coefficient is exactly the difference-in-differences estimate: 6.5246.

Its standard error is 5.729, and the 95% interval is (−4.706, 17.755). The estimate is positive, but it is not statistically distinguishable from zero.

Read the interval, not just the point

A 6.52 estimate with an interval that includes zero is not evidence of no effect; it is evidence that this sample is too noisy to rule out zero. The confidence interval is wide because deposits vary enormously across customers.

The equivalence

The regression does not add a new causal assumption. It reproduces the 2×2 DiD and gives the standard error that the four means alone cannot provide.

the p-value

The book reports p = 0.255. The estimate is positive but compatible with zero at conventional levels.

the mistake

Do not call the campaign ineffective. The data are consistent with effects from −4.7 to +17.8.

04 the assumption

Non-parallel trends

The lines
might not match.

DiD is only as credible as the parallel trends assumption. Pre-period data can show whether the two cities were already diverging.

Suppose Porto Alegre was falling before the campaign while Florianópolis was rising. Then the treated city’s counterfactual trend would be downward, and DiD would overstate the campaign. The book’s illustrative history makes exactly that point: the pre-periods can reveal a violation that the two-period comparison hides.

Aggregated data

With only the four group means, you can still compute the DiD estimate, but not its standard error. Aggregation squashes the customer-level variation that the regression uses.

the test that isn't

Parallel trends is about the counterfactual. Pre-trends are a diagnostic, not a proof; a flat pre-period does not guarantee a parallel post-period.

the alternative

When trends differ, synthetic control builds a comparison from many untreated units. That is the next chapter.

05 key ideas

Carry these forward

Four things
to keep.

  1. DiD is a difference of changes. Treated change minus control change; it does not compare levels and does not assume a flat trend.
  2. Parallel trends is the assumption. The control trend stands in for the treated counterfactual. Pre-periods can make that assumption more or less credible.
  3. The regression is the same estimator. The interaction coefficient equals the 2×2 DiD and adds the standard error.
  4. Noise can dominate. A 6.52 estimate with a CI from −4.7 to 17.8 is not evidence of no effect; it is a wide interval.

The sentence to avoid

“Deposits rose 41 reais, so the billboards worked.” Deposits rose in both cities. The DiD estimate is the part beyond the common trend.

to remember

Plot the trends before you trust the estimate. If the pre-periods already diverge, DiD is borrowing a trend that may not exist.

06 practice

Check yourself

Can you
defend it?

Four short questions. Answer first, then open the field note.

01 Why is before-and-after not enough?

It assumes the outcome would not have changed without the campaign. If deposits are trending up for everyone, the before-after comparison attributes the trend to the campaign.

02 Why is the post cross-section not enough?

It compares Porto Alegre and Florianópolis after the campaign, but the cities have very different baseline levels. The level difference is not a treatment effect.

03 What is the parallel trends assumption?

Without treatment, the treated unit would have followed the same trend as the control. It is an assumption about a counterfactual trend, not a testable fact.

04 What does the regression interaction add?

The same DiD point estimate plus a standard error. It uses the customer-level variation that the four aggregated means discard.
