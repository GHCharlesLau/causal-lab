01 prediction

A mapping, not a cause

Predict the
outcome.

Machine learning estimates a conditional expectation. It is very good at mapping known inputs to an unknown output; it is not, by itself, a causal statement.

This is the bridge to Part II. The question changes from “what is the average effect?” to “who should we treat?” Prediction gives a number; a policy turns that number into a decision.

A prediction is Ê[Y|X] — the expected net value for a customer with region, income, and age. A policy is a rule: engage if the prediction is above zero, or above some other threshold.

prediction → policy pi = Ê[Yi | Xi]    →    engagei = 1{pi > t}

Cross-validation is what makes the evaluation credible. Split the labelled data into training and test sets, fit the rule on one, and evaluate it on the other. Otherwise a flexible model can memorize the training set and look perfect for the wrong reason.

The distinction

Prediction: how much will this customer be worth? Policy: should we engage? The first is an estimate; the second is a decision with consequences.

the trap

A model can be accurate and still useless if the prediction is not attached to a decision that changes an outcome.

the caution

Predicting who is profitable is not the same as knowing who becomes profitable because we engage them. That is a causal question for later chapters.

02 the data

Ten thousand customers

One number
per customer.

Sum the thirty days of transactions, subtract acquisition cost, and you get net value. Then split the labelled data before doing anything else.

The data has 10,000 customers, 30 days of transactions, and three pre-acquisition features: region, income, and age. Net value is the sum of all cash events. The training set has 7,000 customers; the test set has 3,000.

The training mean is −29.17 reais per customer. Treating everyone loses money. The question is whether a rule can select a profitable subset.

Why split first

If you choose the rule after looking at the test set, the test set is no longer a test. It becomes part of training, and the evaluation is optimistic.

the target

Net value is a simplification. It ignores churn timing, maintenance, and the difference between a customer who never buys and one who is between purchases.

the question

Are we predicting who is profitable, or deciding who to acquire? The data can answer the first; the second needs a policy.

03 the policy bench

One feature, one rule

How confident
do you need to be?

The region rule keeps regions whose training average is significantly above zero. Raise the confidence cutoff and fewer regions survive.

For each region, compute the training mean and the lower bound of its 95% interval. Keep the region if the lower bound is above zero. That selects 18 regions and earns 15.57 per test customer. The model policy earns 16.60, only slightly more.

the confidence rule

The lower bound is a multiple-testing hazard: with 50 regions, some will look profitable by chance. The test set is the check.

the comparison

Model 16.60 vs region 15.57. A small gain, but over many customers it can be real money.

04 the model

Gradient boosting

A little better,
a lot more complex.

The model uses all three features and their interactions. It explains 71% of training variance and 69% of test variance.

The book fits a gradient-boosted regression with 400 trees, depth 4, and a learning rate of 0.01. The train R² is 0.7109; the test R² is 0.6939. That gap is modest, which suggests the model is not badly overfit.

The policy engages customers whose predicted net value is above zero. It earns 16.60 per test customer, against 15.57 for the region rule. The gain is real but small; the complexity is much larger.

The model can also be used for a finer policy. Instead of a binary engage / don't engage, split predictions into 20 bands and spend more on the top bands. The decision becomes continuous: how much to invest, not just whether to invest.

The prediction is not the policy

The model outputs a number. The business decision is the threshold, the budget, and the cost of being wrong. None of that is in the R².

the gain

16.60 vs 15.57 is about one real per customer. At a million customers, that is a million reais — and also a million opportunities to get the policy wrong.

the limits

This is a predictive exercise, not a causal one. The model does not tell us what happens if we change who we engage.

05 key ideas

Carry these forward

Four things
to keep.

  1. ML estimates E[Y|X]. It maps known inputs to an unknown output. It does not identify a causal effect on its own.
  2. Cross-validation is non-negotiable. Fit on one partition, judge on another; otherwise a flexible model will flatter itself.
  3. Predictions are inputs to policies. The decision rule, threshold, and cost of errors are separate choices.
  4. Complexity has to earn its keep. The model beats the region rule by about one real per customer; that may or may not justify the machinery.

The sentence to avoid

“The model predicts profit, so engaging the predicted-profitable customers will cause profit.” Prediction is not causation; the next part of the book is about closing that gap.

to remember

Split first. Fit the rule. Judge it on the test set. Then ask what decision the number is supposed to change.

06 practice

Check yourself

Can you
defend it?

Four short questions. Answer first, then open the field note.

01 What is the difference between a prediction and a policy?

A prediction is an estimate of an outcome, usually E[Y|X]. A policy is a decision rule that maps the prediction to an action, such as engaging only if the prediction is above zero.

02 Why is cross-validation necessary?

A flexible model can fit the training set perfectly, including its noise. Evaluating on held-out data estimates how the rule will perform on customers it has not seen.

03 Why did the model only slightly beat the region rule?

The region already carries much of the signal. Income and age add some information, but the incremental predictive gain is small; complexity does not automatically buy accuracy.

04 What causal question does this chapter not answer?

Whether engaging a customer changes their net value. That is a treatment effect, not a prediction; it needs a causal design, which the next chapters develop.
