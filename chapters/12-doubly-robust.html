Doubly robust estimation combines the propensity model and the outcome model.

The propensity score reweights the sample; the outcome model predicts each unit under treatment and control. If either model is right, the estimate stays close. If both are wrong, nothing saves it.

01 two baskets

Don't put all your eggs in one basket

Two models,
two chances.

The propensity model and the outcome model make different assumptions. Doubly robust estimation puts them together so that either one can rescue the estimate.

IPTW relies on the propensity model. Regression adjustment relies on the outcome model. Doubly robust estimation uses both. If the propensity score is right, the weighting part is consistent; if the outcome model is right, the prediction part is consistent. If either is right, the combination is consistent.

That is a genuine insurance policy, not a free lunch: the guarantee is only as good as the statement “at least one of my two models is approximately right.” If both are wrong, the estimate can be biased like any other.

the estimator ψ = 1n Σ [ m1(X) + T(Y − m1(X))e(X) − m0(X) − (1−T)(Y − m0(X))1 − e(X) ]

The first term predicts the treated outcome and corrects the prediction with the weighted residual. The second does the same for the control. The residuals vanish when the outcome model is right; the weighting ratio corrects when it is not.

The insurance policy

Propensity right: the weighted residuals correct the outcome model. Outcome right: the residuals are zero, so the prediction stands. Both wrong: no guarantee.

the intuition

Each observation gets a predicted outcome and a weight. If the prediction is off, the weight corrects it; if the weight is off, the prediction carries it.

the fine print

Doubly robust is not doubly certain. It is one estimate with two assumptions and one escape clause.

The robustness grid

What if
one is wrong?

The book replaces one model at a time with a deliberately bad one. Click a cell and read the estimate.

The “wrong” models are random draws, not a specific misspecification. They are a stress test: if the estimate stays near 0.39 when one model is nonsense, the other model is doing its job.

try all four

Both right, PS wrong, outcome wrong, both wrong. The first three cluster near 0.39; the last has no guarantee.

the catch

The wrong models are random. A real misspecification could be worse than random noise.

03 intervals

Bootstrap uncertainty

The intervals
are wide.

The point estimates are close, but the intervals run from about 0.34 to 0.43. The insurance policy buys robustness, not precision.

The book’s 1,000-sample bootstrap gives a doubly robust ATE of 0.3882 and a 95% interval of (0.3536, 0.4198). Replacing the propensity model with random scores gives 0.3797 and an interval of (0.3381, 0.4336). Replacing the outcome model with random predictions gives 0.3981 and (0.3386, 0.4332).

The intervals overlap heavily. The estimates are close and all near the IPTW estimate of 0.3885 from Chapter 11; this precision does not distinguish them.

The comparison

DR: 0.3882. IPTW: 0.3885. PS regression: 0.3930. PS matching: 0.3890. Four methods, one story; the naive difference was 0.4723.

the width

The interval is about 0.066 wide. A 0.39 effect is meaningful, but the data cannot pin it down to two decimals.

the mistake

Do not read the closeness of the point estimates as proof that both models are right. It is evidence that the estimate is stable under these perturbations.

04 the limits

No free lunch

Both wrong
is still wrong.

The escape clause requires at least one credible model. Overlap, unmeasured confounding, and finite samples still matter.

Doubly robust estimation does not fix positivity. If some treated units have propensity scores where no controls exist, both the weighting and the outcome predictions rely on extrapolation. It does not fix unmeasured confounding either: both models are built from the same observed covariates.

The “wrong” models in the book are random on purpose. A real analyst’s misspecification is rarely random; it can be systematically wrong in the direction that matters. The grid is a stress test, not a guarantee.

Two ways to lose the guarantee

Both models wrong: no consistency. No overlap: no counterfactual to predict or weight toward, even with a perfect model.

The honest sentence

“The doubly robust estimate is 0.388, with a 95% interval of (0.354, 0.420), under the assumption that either the propensity model or the outcome model is approximately right and that the groups overlap.”

the diagnostic

Check overlap and balance first. Doubly robust is not a substitute for a credible design; it is a second line of defence.

the sentence to avoid

“Doubly robust means I only need one model to be right, so I can be careless with both.” The guarantee is only as good as the model you actually got right.

05 key ideas

Carry these forward

Four things
to keep.

  1. Two models, two chances. DR combines a propensity model and an outcome model; consistency survives if either one is right.
  2. The residuals do the work. If the outcome model is wrong, the weighted residuals correct it; if it is right, the residuals are zero.
  3. Robustness is not precision. The DR interval is still about 0.066 wide; the insurance policy does not buy more data.
  4. Both wrong is still wrong. DR does not fix positivity, unmeasured confounding, or a systematically bad model.

The free lunch that isn't

The doubly robust guarantee is conditional on at least one model being approximately right. That is a real assumption, not a technicality.

to remember

Say which model you trust and why. “Doubly robust” is not a substitute for that sentence.

06 practice

Check yourself

Can you
defend it?

Four short questions. Answer first, then open the field note.

01 What makes an estimator doubly robust?

It combines a model for treatment (the propensity score) with a model for the outcome. It is consistent if either model is correctly specified, not only if both are.

02 Which model is doing the work when the propensity model is wrong?

The outcome model. Its predictions m1(X) and m0(X) carry the estimate, while the weighted residuals correct for the bad weights.

03 Why are the DR intervals still wide?

Robustness to model misspecification is not the same as precision. The estimate still depends on 10,391 observations and the variation in the outcome.

04 What happens if both models are wrong?

The doubly robust guarantee disappears. The estimate can be biased, just like a single-model estimator with a misspecified model.
