01 a different question

Prediction vs. cause

What is,
versus what if.

Machine learning is very good at predicting what comes next. Causal questions ask what would have happened under a different decision.

A model can tell a hotel that low prices and low sales tend to appear together. It cannot tell the hotel what would happen if it raised the price. That is a different question — an inverse question, a what-if.

Should a school give every student a tablet? Should a bank change its credit line? Should a hospital give a drug to the next patient? Each question asks about an intervention. The data show us the world under one decision; the causal question asks about a world we did not get to see.

This chapter builds the notation for that missing world. It is short, but it is the grammar for everything that follows: experiments, graphs, regression, matching, instruments, and the modern heterogeneity tools in Part II.

The shift

Prediction asks what is. Causality asks what would be if we acted differently — and then asks what assumptions make the answer credible.

the tablet question

Schools with tablets get higher scores. Is that the tablet — or the tuition those schools can afford?

not a prediction problem

A better predictor of scores is not a better estimate of the tablet’s effect.

02 the two roads

Potential outcomes

One unit.
Two outcomes.

For each unit, define the outcome under treatment and the outcome under control. Only one of them can be observed.

Let Ti be the treatment for unit i: 1 if treated, 0 otherwise. Let Y0i be the outcome unit i would have under control, and Y1i the outcome under treatment. The individual treatment effect is their difference:

individual effect TEi = Y1i − Y0i

We never see both. If unit i is treated, we observe Y1i and Y0i is a counterfactual. If it is untreated, the reverse. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference: the same unit cannot travel both roads.

The book’s four-school table makes the problem concrete. Suppose a causal deity lets us see both potential outcomes. The observed Y column is what we would see; the last is the effect we could compute if we could see both roads.

With both potential outcomes, the average treatment effect is just the average of the individual effects. The average effect on the treated uses only the treated units:

the targets ATE = E[Y1 − Y0]     ATT = E[Y1 − Y0 | T = 1]

The fundamental problem

An individual treatment effect is never identified from one observed outcome. Everything causal is a strategy for identifying an average by making different units comparable.

try it first

Before you reveal the lens: which school do you think has the largest negative effect? Then check the TE column.

read the table

School 1 is untreated, so its observed Y is Y0. School 3 is treated, so its observed Y is Y1. The other cell in each row is missing.

03 association vs. cause

The bias term

The naive gap
is not the effect.

Compare the treated and untreated averages and you get the effect on the treated — plus a term for how different they were before treatment.

Take the observed table at face value. The treated schools average 675; the untreated average 550. The association is +125. But the true ATT is −75. The difference between those two numbers is bias:

association +125
=
ATT −75
+
bias +200

The bias is the difference in untreated outcomes between the groups: the treated schools would have averaged 750 even without tablets; the untreated average 550. They were already 200 points apart. Giving them tablets did not create that gap — tuition, teachers, and neighborhoods did.

In potential-outcome notation, add and subtract the counterfactual control mean for the treated, E[Y0 | T = 1], and regroup:

the decomposition E[Y | T=1] − E[Y | T=0] = E[Y1 − Y0 | T=1] + { E[Y0 | T=1] − E[Y0 | T=0] }

Why this is the whole book

Every identification strategy is an argument that the bias term is zero, or a way to estimate and subtract it. Randomization makes the two groups comparable; matching finds comparable units; regression controls for the confounder; instruments use a nudge that is unrelated to the bias.

So when can association be causation? When the treated and untreated would have had the same untreated outcome. In notation, E[Y0 | T=1] = E[Y0 | T=0]. Then the bias vanishes and the difference in means equals the ATT. That is the exchangeability assumption, and it is the door to Chapter 02.

check the arithmetic

Treated mean: (600 + 750) / 2 = 675. Control mean: (500 + 600) / 2 = 550. Naive gap: +125. ATT: (−200 + 50) / 2 = −75. Bias: 125 − (−75) = 200.

the temptation

“The treated schools score higher, so tablets work.” This is the most common causal mistake in applied work — and the one this book is built to prevent.

04 the way in

Exchangeability

Make the groups
the same on paper.

If treatment is assigned by a coin flip, the treated and untreated are comparable in expectation. Then the naive gap becomes an estimate of the effect.

A randomized experiment is the cleanest version of the idea. Flip a coin for each school. Rich and poor schools have the same chance of getting tablets. The groups may still differ by chance, but not systematically. In expectation, the bias term is zero.

That does not mean every experiment gives the right answer. A finite sample can be unlucky; a broken randomization can be biased; non-compliance can dilute the effect. Chapter 02 takes the coin seriously and shows how to estimate and quantify the result.

For now, keep the grammar: a causal quantity is defined with potential outcomes; an estimate is credible when the design makes the observed groups comparable on the untreated road.

05 key ideas

Carry these forward

Four things
to keep.

  1. Potential outcomes are the language. Every unit has a treated and an untreated outcome; we observe one.
  2. The fundamental problem is missing data. The counterfactual is not a modeling inconvenience; it is structurally absent.
  3. Association = ATT + bias. The naive gap is only causal when the bias term is zero.
  4. Design beats arithmetic. No amount of regression can rescue a comparison whose groups were selected on the outcome’s untreated level.

Common trap

Adding more variables does not automatically reduce bias. Some variables are mediators; some are colliders; some are proxies for the treatment. Chapter 07 makes this precise.

to remember

“Compared with what?” is the first question of every causal estimate.

06 practice

Check yourself

Can you
defend it?

Four short questions. Answer first, then open the field note.

01 Why can’t we compute an individual treatment effect from the observed table?

Because each unit contributes only one potential outcome. For a treated school we observe Y1 but not Y0; for a control school we observe Y0 but not Y1. The missing cell is the counterfactual, not a data-entry error.

02 What is the difference between the ATE and the ATT, and when are they equal?

The ATE averages over everyone; the ATT averages over the treated only. They are equal when the treatment effect is the same for treated and untreated units. Under random assignment the groups are comparable in expectation, but that alone does not force the two effects to be equal.

03 The naive gap is +125 and the ATT is −75. What is the bias, and what does it mean?

Bias = 125 − (−75) = +200. In this example, treated schools would have averaged 750 without tablets, while untreated schools averaged 550. The +200 is selection: richer schools were already different before the treatment.

04 What condition would make the difference in means equal the ATT?

Exchangeability of the untreated outcomes: E[Y0 | T=1] = E[Y0 | T=0]. Then the bias term is zero, and the observed difference in means equals the average effect on the treated.
